# 19 — Exit Plan

Build it saleable from day one. Not because you intend to sell, but because
**the properties that make a business saleable are exactly the properties that
make it low-touch** — documented, automated, transferable, and not dependent on
you.

If you never sell, you still get the benefit. If you do, the work is already
done.

## What a business like this sells for

Small profitable SaaS businesses typically transact at a multiple of annual
profit (SDE), commonly in the **3×–5×** range, with a wide spread. The
multiple, not the revenue, is where the money is — and the multiple is decided
by a handful of factors you control.

| Higher multiple | Lower multiple |
| --- | --- |
| 24+ months of history | Under 12 months |
| Growing, and growth is explainable | Flat or declining |
| Churn under 3% | Churn above 6% |
| Diversified acquisition | One channel, or one algorithm |
| No customer over 10% of revenue | Concentrated revenue |
| Documented, transferable operations | It lives in the founder's head |
| Clean books, separate accounts | Mixed personal and business finances |
| Boring, maintainable stack | Exotic or unmaintained technology |
| Owner works under 10 hrs/week | Owner is the product |

**Read the last row.** The thing you asked for in the first place is also the
single biggest driver of sale value. A business consuming 10 hours a week sells
for meaningfully more than the identical business consuming 40 — because the
buyer is purchasing freedom, and you are selling proof that it exists.

## Building for transferability from day one

Costs almost nothing while you're building; expensive to retrofit later.

- [ ] **Everything in the business's name**, not yours — domains, hosting,
      payment provider, software accounts, social handles
- [ ] **Never use your personal email** for a business account
- [ ] **Business bank account, separate, from day one**
- [ ] **Clean books from month one.** Reconstructing two years of mixed
      transactions during due diligence is where deals die
- [ ] **Monthly metrics spreadsheet, maintained.** A buyer will ask for 24
      months of MRR, churn and customer counts. Having it instantly is a
      credibility signal in itself
- [ ] **Documentation as though someone else runs it** — this whole pack, plus
      `14-runbooks.md`
- [ ] **No personal brand dependence.** If the business is "Jordan's tool", it
      is worth less. Build a product brand
- [ ] **No handshake arrangements.** Every supplier and contractor relationship
      in writing, and assignable
- [ ] **You own your code and content outright.** Any contractor work needs a
      written IP assignment. Check the licence of every dependency
- [ ] **No customer above 10% of revenue.** Concentration is the most common
      valuation discount

## Diligence pack — keep it current

Assemble it once, refresh quarterly. Half a day now; weeks of scramble later.

- [ ] 24 months of P&L and monthly MRR
- [ ] Customer list with signup date, plan, and revenue (anonymised until
      exclusivity)
- [ ] Cohort retention and churn history
- [ ] Traffic and acquisition by channel
- [ ] The full technology and vendor stack, with monthly costs
- [ ] Every recurring cost and contract
- [ ] Terms of service, privacy policy, subprocessor list
- [ ] Any trade marks or IP registrations
- [ ] The runbooks
- [ ] An honest list of known issues and technical debt

That last item is counter-intuitive and worth doing. Buyers discover problems
during diligence; disclosing them up front converts a discount into trust.

## When to consider selling

- Growth has plateaued and you have no appetite to change that
- Something else has your attention, and the business is coasting
- A strategic buyer approaches — a competitor, or someone selling to the same
  ICP
- Interest has genuinely gone (see risk R2 in `18`). **A business you have
  stopped caring about loses value every month you hold it.** Selling at that
  point is the rational choice, not a defeat

## Where these sell

- **Marketplaces** — Acquire.com, MicroAcquire, Flippa. Widest reach; expect
  tyre-kickers, and a listing fee or success fee
- **Brokers** — for larger deals; typically 10–15% commission
- **Direct** — competitors, or companies serving the same customers. Often the
  best price, because they buy your customer list as much as your code
- **Your own customers** — occasionally the best outcome for everyone

## The alternative: don't sell

A business at $5–10k MRR taking 10 hours a week is, in cash terms, roughly
equivalent to a portfolio far larger than any realistic sale price — and it
appreciates. The sale price is 3–5 years of profit; the business, maintained,
pays indefinitely.

Sell when you have stopped wanting to run it. Hold when you haven't. Do not
sell because a number sounds large — compare it honestly against the years of
income it replaces.
